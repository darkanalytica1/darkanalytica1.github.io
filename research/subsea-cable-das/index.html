Can Norway see what happens to its Arctic cables?The stretch where the Svalbard cable failed is the stretch it cannot hear.

Two fibre cables carry almost all of Svalbard's data to the mainland, including the downlinks of SvalSat, a ground station far enough north to see polar-orbiting satellites on every orbit. One of them failed in January 2022 on the edge of the continental shelf. Using open vessel-tracking data, the published fault record and the physics of repeatered cables, this investigation reconstructs who crosses that route, and what the cable itself could have noticed.

01 · The linkWhy this cable matters

The Svalbard Undersea Cable System was laid in 2004 as two separate cables from Longyearbyen to the Norwegian mainland, about 2,714 km of cable in total, each segment carrying eight fibre pairs through twenty repeaters.4 The operator, state-owned Space Norway, built it as a redundant pair: the two cables lie roughly 5 to 10 km apart on the seabed.7

Its most demanding user sits on a plateau above Longyearbyen. SvalSat, the ground station run by Kongsberg Satellite Services, is far enough north to see a polar-orbiting satellite on every orbit, about 14 passes a day, which is why meteorological, Earth-observation and navigation programmes put antennas there.8 NASA, NOAA, ESA and EUMETSAT operate their own installations at the site, and five of its antennas were built for Galileo.8 The US Geological Survey names the undersea fibre as the data link that lets Landsat imagery downloaded on Svalbard reach its archive in South Dakota “in a matter of hours”.8

That makes the cable a single point of national and allied dependence in a place Russia also considers strategically important. It is also, physically, a 1,375 km strand lying across some of the most intensively fished waters in the Arctic.

02 · The incident7 January 2022

At 04:10 CET on Friday 7 January 2022, one of the two cables failed. Space Norway later said the analysis showed a shunt fault, a break in the insulation that cut power to some of the repeaters; traffic ran on the second cable, and redundancy was restored on 18 January by feeding power to the damaged cable from the other end.7 According to Space Norway, as summarised by the Norwegian Institute of International Affairs, the break lay somewhere between 130 and 230 km from Longyearbyen, in an area where the seabed suddenly drops to as deep as 2,700 m.9 The operator located it the way repeatered cables are always located: by feeding power from the Svalbard end and seeing which repeaters came back, since the repeaters, in its words, are “approximately 100 kilometers apart”.9

The cable was only recovered and permanently repaired in June 2023, by the cable ship Cable Vigilance, after a search of about 40 hours for the damaged section.6 Police photographs published by NRK in May 2024 show the outer jacket peeled away and a gap in the steel armour. Experts interviewed by NRK described “pinching” or crushing damage consistent with an object dragged over or along the cable, such as a trawl door, and the police technical report named pinching damage as a possible cause.6

NRK had earlier reported that a Russian trawler crossed the Svalbard cable more than 140 times, more than a dozen of them before the damage; the shipowners denied involvement.6 Police first said they believed human activity was the cause, then dropped the investigation for lack of evidence.6 Nine months earlier, on 3 April 2021, the Lofoten-Vesterålen ocean observatory off northern Norway had gone dark when about 4.2 km of its cable was pulled away; researchers later found some 3 km of it up to 11 km from its original position.5

Neither case produced a public attribution. In both, the evidence that did exist came from vessel tracking and from the damaged cable itself, recovered long after the event.

03 · Original dataWho crosses the cable

Norway's Coastal Administration publishes historical AIS positions for the Norwegian economic zone and the protection zone around Svalbard under an open licence.10 For this investigation we queried every position along the cable corridor and counted each time a vessel's track crossed the route line (method below). Two samples: the 13 days before the 2022 fault, restricted to route km 80 to 280, and a year-long baseline of 26 days spaced a fortnight apart through 2025, along the full route.

In the 13 days before the fault, 33 vessels crossed the route 281 times in that 200 km window. 231 crossings, 82 %, were by Russian-flagged vessels, almost all of them trawlers and fish-factory ships working the shelf edge; the most frequent, the trawler Nothern Eagle (IMO 9128348, spelled so in its AIS record), crossed 37 times, followed by Nord Tral (26) and Barentchevo More (23). 96 crossings fell inside the reported fault zone itself, 75 of them Russian-flagged. In the final 24 hours before the fault there were 26 crossings. The densest traffic sits between km 220 and 250, on the slope where the shelf gives way to deep water, straddling the southern edge of the fault zone.

These numbers describe exposure, not a culprit. Crossing a cable is legal, trawlers on the shelf edge cross it all the time, and this short window does not show which vessel, if any, caused the damage. What the data shows is the scale of the problem: any system meant to notice interference on this stretch has to separate one deliberate act from hundreds of routine passes.

Was that winter unusual? To check, the same query was run for the same 13-day window, 25 December to 7 January, in each of the four following winters.

The answer is no, and then something changed. The winter after the fault looked almost the same, with 273 crossings, 255 of them Russian-flagged. From the winter of 2023/24 crossings in this window fall by about three quarters, to a handful of vessels, and in 2025/26 there were six crossings in total, none by a Russian-flagged vessel. This investigation cannot say why. Candidate explanations include where the fish were, reduced quotas, vessels no longer transmitting AIS, and changes in how the data are collected; none has been tested here. What it does show is that January 2022 was ordinary for its time: the cable failed during a normal season of heavy trawling, which is exactly the background against which a deliberate act would be hardest to see.

The year-round 2025 sample gives the other half of the picture: 758 crossings by 296 distinct vessels on 26 sampled days, only 9 % of them Russian-flagged. 79 % of those crossings happen within the first repeater span at either end, in the approaches to Isfjorden and Andøya, where local ferries, supply ships and coastal fishing concentrate. The traffic that a shore sensor could see is mostly harmless and close to land; the traffic over the slope and deep water, 21 % of the total, is where the cable is deaf.

04 · The physicsWhat the fibre itself could hear

A telecom fibre can be turned into a sensor. Distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) sends laser pulses into a fibre and reads the light scattered back by microscopic imperfections; vibration changes that backscatter, so every few metres of glass becomes a microphone. On a 120 km fibre on Svalbard itself, researchers recorded for 44 days in 2020, tracked a ship about 86 km along the fibre and detected whale calls out to 95 km.2

Long cables, however, need optical amplifiers every few tens of kilometres, and ordinary repeaters amplify in one direction only. The backscattered light from beyond the first repeater never gets back to shore.3 Commercial interrogators reach about 171 km on bare fibre; on a repeatered cable the limit is the first span.3 The repeater spacing on the Svalbard cable is not published. The public system description gives twenty repeaters on the 1,375 km segment, which with even spacing means a span of about 65 km; Space Norway has described the spacing as roughly 100 km.4,9 On the first reading an interrogator at each landing hears about 131 km, 9.5 % of the route; on the second, about 200 km, 14.5 %.

The fault zone sits entirely in the part no shore interrogator can reach. That conclusion holds under either spacing: even with a first span of 120 km the heard stretch stops short of km 130. Two approaches do reach the whole length. Loopback paths built into the repeaters let DAS be run span by span; a range-scalable system using repeater loopbacks has been demonstrated over 2,227 km, but it is not yet a standard option on commercial cables.3 Polarisation sensing reads disturbances from the ordinary data signal and has detected earthquakes and ocean swell along a 10,000 km cable between Los Angeles and Chile; in that form it measures the cable end to end, and placing a disturbance along the route remains a research problem.11

05 · GeometryDepth and access

Depth is what separates the two threats. On the shelf, where the route runs at a few hundred metres, trawl gear and anchors reach the seabed routinely. Beyond the slope the route lies at 2,000 m and more; sampled GEBCO depths along the public route put about 94 % of the deep water, everything below 1,000 m, in the stretch no shore interrogator can hear, and that share stays between 89 and 97 % across plausible span layouts.

At those depths only specialised assets operate. Russia's deep-sea directorate GUGI fields the Rus and Konsul submersibles, rated to 6,000 m and tended by the research ship Yantar; the nuclear-powered Losharik, damaged by fire in 2019, is reported to have resumed sea trials but its status is unclear.12 The operation disclosed in September 2026, in which British, Norwegian and US forces tracked and confronted GUGI submersibles near Svalbard, is the first public case of those assets rehearsing against these cables.1 On this route every metre of seabed they could reach unobserved is deaf to the cable.

The Baltic cases show how the problem usually ends. The container ship NewNew Polar Bear dragged its anchor across the Balticconnector pipeline in October 2023, and China later acknowledged the ship was at fault. The tanker Eagle S dragged an anchor for around 90 km on 25 December 2024; a Helsinki district court threw the case out in October 2025 for lack of jurisdiction, and the Court of Appeal reversed that ruling in August 2026.13 In every case, the evidence was vessel tracking plus physical recovery, and attribution took months or never came. The cables themselves reported only that they had stopped working.

07 · The next cableWhat Norway is building

The 2004 system is nearing the end of its design life. Norway has approved Arctic Way, a new 2,350 km system from Bodø with branches to Jan Mayen and Svalbard, built by SubCom for about NOK 2.8 billion and due in service in 2028.14 The defence estates agency is building the Jan Mayen landing station alongside a new armed forces station on the island.15 None of the public announcements mentions sensing on the cable itself.14,15 That may simply not be disclosed. It is, however, the decision that matters: loopback paths in the repeaters or a fibre pair reserved for sensing have to be specified before the repeaters are manufactured, not added afterwards.

08 · AssessmentWhat we know, and what we do not

We knowOne Svalbard cable failed on 7 January 2022 at 130 to 230 km from Longyearbyen; it was repaired in June 2023; the police case was dropped. Russian-flagged vessels, almost all of them fishing, crossed the route around it (km 80 to 280) 231 times in the 13 days before the fault, a level matched the following winter and not since.
The evidence indicatesThe damage is consistent with gear dragged along the seabed. The fault lies beyond the reach of any shore-based acoustic interrogator on this cable design.
We assessWith shore-based DAS the existing cables cannot observe their own deep route or the 2022 fault zone. Only sensing designed into the new cable can close that gap, and only if it is specified before manufacture.
UnknownThe exact route, the repeater positions, why shelf-edge traffic in the open AIS record collapsed after 2022/23, whether Norway already monitors the cable by other means, and whether DAS could detect a slow submersible at 2,000 m at all.

The cheapest of these is also the most neglected. The crossings in Figure 2 took minutes to compute from public data. A standing watch on vessel behaviour over the corridor, slow tracks, repeated passes, AIS gaps, would not prove intent, but it would turn the next fault from a mystery into a short list.

Method

AIS positions were retrieved from the Norwegian Coastal Administration's Kystdatahuset open API (NLOD licence) in bounding boxes around 28 route segments, per day, at speeds of 0.3 kn or more. For each vessel, consecutive positions less than 30 minutes apart were joined into track segments and tested for intersection with the route line. The route is the approximate public line scaled to the official 1,375 km; the real cables are two lines 5 to 10 km apart, so counts measure crossings of the corridor, not of either cable. Fishing vessels under 15 m and vessels not transmitting AIS are absent. Coverage figures assume evenly spaced repeaters; the sensitivity to that assumption is published with the code.
