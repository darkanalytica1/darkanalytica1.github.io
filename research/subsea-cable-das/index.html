A telecom fibre can work as a long chain of microphones. On a repeatered subsea cable, that only works up to the first repeater from each shore. The deep middle, where an attacker would work, stays deaf.

01 · The signalA rehearsal near the Arctic cables

On 10 September, Reuters reported that a joint British, Norwegian and US operation tracked and confronted Russian deep-sea submersibles from the GUGI directorate near Svalbard, during what officials described as a test of a capability to disable undersea cables covertly.1 Two fibre cables of about 1,400 km link Svalbard to the Norwegian mainland and lie as deep as 2,700 m.1

The question here is narrow and defensive: could a cable like this at least hear something happening along its own route?

02 · What a fibre can hearThe cable as a sensor

Distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) sends laser pulses into a fibre and reads the light scattered back by tiny imperfections in the glass. Vibrations change that backscatter, so each few metres of fibre becomes a sensor. On a 120 km fibre between Longyearbyen and Ny-Ålesund, researchers recorded for 44 days in 2020, tracked a ship about 86 km along the fibre and detected whale calls out to 95 km.2

03 · The constraintThe first repeater is a one-way gate

Long cables need optical amplifiers (repeaters) every few tens of kilometres. Ordinary repeaters amplify in one direction only, so the backscatter from beyond the first repeater never reaches the interrogator on shore.3 Commercial DAS units reach up to about 171 km without amplification,3 but on a repeatered cable the limit is the first span, not the instrument.

Each of the two Svalbard segments is about 1,375 km and 1,339 km long, with twenty repeaters each.4 The span plan is not public. Assuming even spacing, each span is about 65.5 km.

Calculated · both landingsAn interrogator at each end hears about 131 km of the 1,375 km segment, 9.5 %. The longest stretch no shore interrogator can hear is about 1,244 km. From one landing only, coverage falls to 65 km, 4.8 %.

04 · The findingThe ears are in shallow water

The heard stretches sit next to the landings. On the Svalbard side the first 250 km run over the shelf at a few hundred metres; the route then drops past 2,000 m. Sampling GEBCO 2020 depths along the public route, about 94 % of the route deeper than 1,000 m falls in the deaf stretch.5 The share stays between 89 % and 97 % if the shore spans are anywhere between 30 and 120 km, so the conclusion does not depend on the even-spacing assumption.

A better interrogator does not change this. Enhanced-scattering fibre has pushed DAS to 200.6 km on unrepeatered systems,6 which is useful on short links, but the first repeater still stops the returning light.

05 · AssessmentDecided at specification

We assess that a repeatered cable like this cannot guard its own deep route with shore-based DAS today. The options that reach the deep middle, loopback paths in the repeaters or a reserved fibre pair designed for sensing, have to be chosen before the repeaters are built. Norway plans a new cable to Svalbard and Jan Mayen by 2028;1 that is the cheapest moment to decide whether it should listen to its own route.

06 · LimitsWhat this does not show

This bounds where the fibre can listen. It does not show that DAS would detect a quiet submersible at 2,000 m; that depends on noise, coupling and the signal, and remains open. The route is the approximate public line scaled to the official segment length, the repeater positions are assumed, and the deepest sampled point (2,375 m) is shallower than the reported 2,700 m because the route is approximate. Nothing here describes how any weapon works.
