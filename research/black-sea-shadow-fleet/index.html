Positions that lieThe Black Sea's impossible ship positions are written on the Russian coast, and they land on every ship. Off Romania, the listed vessels' tracks hold up.

Shadow-fleet tankers are said to fake where they are. To test that for the waters off Romania, this investigation took six months of open vessel-tracking data for the whole Black Sea, 6.1 million positions from 5,904 vessels, and checked every one against physics, against land, and against the ships around it.

01 · The questionWhy look at Romanian waters

Romania's largest energy project sits in the middle of its exclusive economic zone. Neptun Deep, developed by OMV Petrom and Romgaz, covers a 7,500 km² block about 160 km offshore in 100 to 1,000 m of water, with ten wells, subsea systems, an unmanned shallow-water platform and a gas pipeline to Tuzla; first gas is planned for 2027.12 This summer the war reached it. On 11 August Romanian navy divers destroyed two Russian Gerbera drones found drifting about 90 nautical miles off Constanța, near the project, after the crew of a civilian vessel working there reported them.13 On 20 August an F-16 strafed an explosive-laden naval drone near the platform; the defence ministry confirmed it carried a charge and the president attributed the incident to Russia.1416

At the same time, the tankers and cargo ships Western governments call Russia's shadow fleet cross the same sea every day, and they have a reputation for lying about where they are. In 2023 SkyTruth showed four tankers broadcasting elaborate false tracks near the coasts of Bulgaria and Romania while radar satellites placed them near the Kerch Strait, about 400 miles away.10

So the question for this investigation was concrete. Which listed vessels show position reports that break the laws of physics, in or near the Romanian EEZ, and how close do their tracks come to Romanian offshore infrastructure? The answer turned out to sit mostly on the other side of the sea, and to say more about the Russian coast than about the ships.

02 · The fleetSix lists, one fleet, little agreement

There is no single list of the shadow fleet. There are sanctions lists, each with its own legal basis, and there is Ukraine's military intelligence directorate (GUR), which publishes a register of vessels it says serve Russia's war economy. Consolidating the vessel entries of the EU, UK, US, Canadian, Swiss and GUR lists by IMO number, as republished by OpenSanctions on 28 September 2026, and keeping only Russia-related programmes, gives 1,467 vessels.38

Figure 2 · Russia-linked vessels on each list, of 1,467

Figure 2. OpenSanctions consolidation of official lists, 28 September 2026, vessels keyed by IMO number. GUR is an intelligence register, not a legal sanction. A vessel can be on several lists.

The lists overlap far less than the phrase "sanctioned tanker" suggests. 514 vessels, a third of the total, appear on the GUR register and on no Western list at all. 794 are on GUR but not on the EU list, and only 190 are on all three of the EU, UK and US lists. The fleet is old and fluid: the median vessel was built in 2005, 62 % are more than twenty years old, 1,238 have changed flag and 1,198 have changed name at least once. 1,077 are tankers.

That matters for anything that follows. "Listed" in this article means on at least one of the six lists; "Western-sanctioned" means on at least one of EU, UK, US, Canada or Switzerland. The distinction turns out to decide who is actually in Romanian waters.

03 · Original dataSix months of the whole sea, from Norway

The obvious data sources for this question are closed. Global Fishing Watch's API needs an account; aisstream needs a key; commercial providers charge. One free, open source turned out to cover the Black Sea. The Norwegian Coastal Administration publishes historical AIS under the Norwegian open licence, and states that its open stream includes satellite AIS.12 Its archive holds Black Sea positions from 8 September 2025 to 14 March 2026, and none before or after; that window is the study period.

Every two days, the whole sea was queried, lon 27.2 to 41.9° E, lat 40.8 to 47.4° N; each query returns roughly the previous 48 hours, so coverage is close to continuous. The result: 6,101,872 positions from 5,904 distinct vessels, of which 223 broadcast an MMSI that appears on one of the lists (316,916 positions). Satellite AIS is sparse compared with coastal receivers; a vessel may report every few minutes or every few hours.

Each track was then tested against three physical facts. Ships do not sail on land, so any position more than about 3 km inside the coastline counts as inland, with navigable rivers excluded. Merchant ships do not exceed about 25 knots, so two consecutive fixes at least 20 km apart that imply more than 50 knots count as an impossible jump. And unrelated ships do not stand on the same inland spot, so inland points shared by many vessels are counted separately. The code is published as ais-spoof-check.

04 · FindingWhere positions break

Impossible positions are not rare. 1,674 vessels, 28 % of all vessels seen, reported at least one position far inland during the six months, and 1,844 reported at least one impossible jump. But they are not spread across the sea. Figure 1, in its default view, shows where the inland positions fall: a dense cluster on the Russian coast between the Kerch Strait and Novorossiysk, a second one on Crimea, a thinner scatter along the Caucasus coast and near Odesa, and nothing at all in the Romanian EEZ.

In the Kerch, Taman and Novorossiysk area, 1,166 of the 1,374 unlisted vessels that entered it, 85 %, reported at least one inland position. So did 149 of 175 listed vessels (85 %).

More telling than the number is the geometry. The 3,005 distinct inland points are not scattered the way random errors would be. The same few coordinates recur across hundreds of ships. One point on the Taman peninsula north-west of Anapa, at 45.12° N 37.02° E, was reported by 644 different vessels. Another, at 44.58° N 38.11° E, sits 474 m above sea level in the hills east of Gelendzhik, about 8 km from the town's airport, and was reported by 607. Of the 40 most-shared inland points, 33 lie more than 20 m above sea level in the GEBCO terrain model, which rules out a mis-drawn coastline as the explanation.11

Hundreds of unrelated ships reporting the same spot on a hillside is not a transponder fault. It is what a receiver does when a stronger, false satellite signal tells it where it is.

05 · InterpretationWhose lie is it?

This is where the shadow-fleet story usually goes wrong. An impossible position in the Black Sea is easy to find, and easy to read as a ship hiding something. The data allows a direct test: compare listed vessels with every other vessel in the same water. If the anomaly follows the ship, listed vessels should show it far more often. If it belongs to the place, both groups should show it at similar rates.

The result is the second answer. Across the whole sea, listed vessels look far worse: 72 % of them reported an inland position, against 27 % of everyone else. But that comparison is misleading, because listed vessels spend their time where the false positions are. Within each area the gap all but disappears. Near Kerch and Novorossiysk the shares are identical: 85 % of listed vessels and 85 % of all others. Western-sanctioned vessels there, the strictest subgroup, come out at 85 % too (41 of 48). On the Crimean coast the figures are 53 % and 46 %.

A grain carrier waiting for the Kerch Strait, a Turkish coaster and a sanctioned tanker at Novorossiysk all report the same inland points. The false position belongs to the coast, not to the ship.

What writes it? The pattern matches what C4ADS documented in 2019: 9,883 suspected GNSS spoofing incidents affecting 1,311 civilian vessels, concentrated around Russian strategic sites, with a likely transmitter on this very coast near Gelendzhik.9 EASA's current safety bulletin on GNSS interference, revised in July 2026, lists the Black Sea among the affected areas and describes spoofing as counterfeit satellite signals that make receivers compute an incorrect position.11 This investigation cannot see the transmitter; attributing the inland points to shore-based spoofing is an inference, but it is the only explanation consistent with hundreds of unrelated receivers reporting identical coordinates.

Panel C of Figure 3 shows the exception worth keeping in mind. SPARTA IV, a Russian-flagged cargo ship the United States sanctioned in 2022 as property of SK-Yug, a subsidiary of the Russian defence ministry's shipping company Oboronlogistika, spent 29 November to 2 December 2025 reporting positions on the runway area of Simferopol airport, inland Crimea, at speeds up to 60 knots.58 Its real position is not in the data. Twenty-nine minutes after one airport fix, a single position appeared in the Romanian EEZ, 262 km west, and then the track went back to the airport. Positions at airports are a known signature of spoofing around protected sites.9 Whether this was the coast's lie, the ship's, or a decoding error, one of only two Western-sanctioned vessels that show up inside Romania's EEZ in six months of data does so through a position that cannot be true. The other, the products tanker SEA MARINE 1, crossed the southern edge of the zone in March 2026 with an ordinary track.

06 · Off RomaniaWho is actually there

The Romanian EEZ is busy: 1,478 distinct vessels reported positions inside it during the study period. None of them reported an inland position inside the zone. That does not make it clean, and section 06b explains why.

Only 13 listed vessels entered the zone, and the most frequent of them are not what the phrase "shadow fleet" suggests.

The three most frequent are crude tankers on the GUR register only, on no Western list. Their tracks form a shuttle between the Novorossiysk terminals and anchorages about 13 km east of Constanța; ALATAU, in panel D of Figure 3, arrives, waits three weeks at anchor and leaves. The Novorossiysk terminal of the Caspian Pipeline Consortium exports mainly Kazakh crude, and the Petromidia refinery north of Constanța belongs to Rompetrol, a subsidiary of Kazakhstan's KazMunayGas.18 That fits a Kazakh-crude supply route, but this investigation did not verify cargoes or charterers, and nothing here suggests the trade breaches any sanction. The rest are general-cargo ships calling at Constanța and Sulina.

On proximity, the answer is plain. No listed vessel came within 25 km of the approximate Neptun Deep position; the closest, S Waters One, passed about 47 km away. 44 unlisted vessels did come within 25 km, 13 of them within 10 km, which is what the construction and supply traffic of a project under development looks like. The listed vessels' path runs along the Constanța approach, well west of the block.

06b · FindingShips in two places

The Romanian EEZ has no inland positions, but it has something stranger. 198 vessels produced impossible jumps between the Romanian EEZ and the waters off Crimea, 739 jumps in all. For 73 of them the Romanian EEZ is where most of their positions fall; 72 more are mostly seen at the Bosphorus approaches. Panel B of Figure 3 shows a typical case: a Panama-registered ship at anchor off Sulina, inside the Romanian EEZ, whose identity keeps reporting positions off Sevastopol and western Crimea, 250 to 280 km east, sometimes at 50 knots, and then snaps back to the anchorage minutes later.

The pattern comes in bursts: 86 % of these jumps fall on 11 days, when dozens of ships show it at once, and the Crimean-side positions repeat across different ships. Only 4 of the 198 are listed vessels. This is the geometry of a 2021 incident, when AIS showed the British and Dutch warships HMS Defender and HNLMS Evertsen sailing towards Sevastopol while they were moored in Odesa.19 Here the ships whose identities appear off Crimea are ordinary merchant ships lying off the Romanian coast. What produces the second position, false AIS messages transmitted near Crimea, identity cloning, or a processing error in the satellite data, cannot be settled from this data. It is the clearest case in this study of positions that lie about ships in Romanian waters, and it has nothing to do with the shadow fleet.

That the listed vessels' own tracks off Romania hold up is a real finding, with a limit. The kind of lie SkyTruth caught off Romania in 2023 was a smooth, plausible false track, detectable only by checking it against radar satellite images.10 This study used no radar imagery. The listed vessels' tracks off Romania pass every physical test, and none shows the repeated-geometry signature of a synthetic track, but that proves consistency, not truth.

07 · ContextWhat port-state control sees

Positions are one check; inspections are another. The Black Sea Memorandum of Understanding on port-state control publishes every detention. Scraping all 57 months from January 2022 to September 2026 gives 1,103 detentions.7

Figure 5 · Detentions by port state, 2022 to September 2026 · brass: vessels now on a Russia-linked list

Figure 5. Black Sea MoU BSIS public detention list, joined by IMO number to the list table. List status as of 28 September 2026, not as of the detention.

78 of those detentions, of 61 different ships, involved vessels that are on a Russia-linked list today. 63 of them were made in Russian ports; Russian inspectors detain listed ships more often than anyone, which says more about the condition of an ageing fleet than about enforcement. Romanian ports made 4 such detentions out of 223, all in Constanța. One was the tanker BLACKSEA EAGLE, detained on 9 September 2024 and released a week later; the United Kingdom added it to its sanctions list on 21 July 2025, ten months after it left Constanța.47 Port-state control can hold a ship for its condition, not for its customers.

08 · AssessmentWhat we know, and what we do not

We knowBetween 8 September 2025 and 14 March 2026, 1,674 of 5,904 vessels in open AIS reported positions far inland, concentrated on the Russian coast between Kerch and Novorossiysk and on Crimea; single inland points were reported by up to 644 different vessels. No vessel reported an inland position inside the Romanian EEZ.
The evidence indicatesWithin the same sea area, listed and unlisted vessels show inland positions at similar rates. The false positions belong to places, not to ships. Off Romania, the most frequent listed vessels are GUR-listed tankers shuttling between Novorossiysk and anchorages off Constanța, and none came within 25 km of Neptun Deep.
We assessWith medium confidence, the inland clusters are the footprint of shore-based GNSS spoofing on the Russian and Crimean coasts. Any monitoring system that flags "spoofing ships" from AIS anomalies without a control group will mostly flag the coast, and will flag innocent ships that queue there.
UnknownWhat produces the bursts in which ships anchored off Romania also report positions off Crimea; whether any listed vessel off Romania broadcast a plausible false track (needs radar imagery); what happened after March 2026, including during the August drone incidents; the cargoes of the Constanța shuttles; the exact positions of Neptun Deep installations.

The practical lesson for Romania is less dramatic than a shadow tanker loitering over a gas field, and more useful. The waters off Constanța are, in this data, the part of the Black Sea where positions still hold. Six hundred kilometres east, the coast itself rewrites where ships are, and ships leaving Novorossiysk bring that record with them. A watch on Romanian infrastructure built on open AIS has to model that: compare every vessel with its neighbours, treat shared false points as a property of the coast, and keep radar imagery for the one case AIS cannot settle, a ship that lies smoothly.

MethodHow this was done

Lists. OpenSanctions FtM exports of 28 September 2026 for the EU Official Journal and sanctions map, UK, US OFAC SDN, Canada, Switzerland and Ukraine GUR; vessels keyed by IMO number; Russia-related programmes only (Iran, North Korea and Venezuela programmes excluded).34568 AIS. Kystdatahuset bounding-box queries, whole Black Sea, every second day; tracks cut at each query window, no inference across windows. Listed vessels matched by the MMSI given on the lists. Inland. Natural Earth 10 m land rasterised at 0.01°, eroded three cells (about 2.5 to 3.3 km) so berths and anchorages never count; lower Danube, Dnieper-Bug and Don excluded; top 40 shared points checked against GEBCO 2020 terrain height. Impossible jump. Consecutive fixes at least 20 km apart, at least 60 s apart, implying more than 50 knots. Areas. Fixed boxes plus the Romanian EEZ polygon from Marine Regions. Port-state control. BSIS public detention list, all 57 months scraped through the public interface with month validation. Full scripts, raw inputs and a claim ledger are in the research package.

Limits. Open Black Sea AIS exists in this source only from 8 September 2025 to 14 March 2026. Satellite AIS drops and corrupts messages, so single outliers are not evidence of anything; this is why the analysis rests on shared points and on the listed-versus-everyone comparison. Vessels are matched by broadcast MMSI, which ships can change. The Neptun Deep point is approximate. Absence of physical anomalies does not prove a track is true.
